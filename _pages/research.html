I am an experimental physicist studying how structure, dynamics, and mechanical response emerge from disorder in amorphous materials. My research aims to identify the microscopic mechanisms that govern structural organization, relaxation, aging, and transformation in glasses.

Current work is centered on metallic glasses, with particular emphasis on:

  • local structure and its connection to dynamics
  • local elastic properties and aspects of heterogeneity
  • the evolution of the amorphous state through physical aging and rejuvenation, and its connection to crystallization
  • the relationship between microscopic structure and macroscopic properties

My broader research interests include the response of molecular and metallic glass-formers to external stimuli such as time, temperature, electric and magnetic fields, and microgravity. I am also interested in how the amorphous structure is influenced by the route of synthesis, for example through liquid quenching or layer deposition.

The experimental work combines established materials-characterization methods, including differential scanning calorimetry, X-ray diffraction, electron microscopy, and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, with advanced techniques capable of resolving structure and dynamics across a broad range of length and time scales. These include synchrotron-based coherent scattering and X-ray photon correlation spectroscopy (XPCS), four-dimensional scanning transmission electron microscopy (4D-STEM), and fast-scanning calorimetry (FSC). High-throughput nanoindentation in the elastic regime provides complementary access to spatial variations in local elastic properties, both under ambient conditions and under thermal stimulation. Current work also explores interdisciplinary approaches, as for example elastomechanical manipulation during coherent scattering experiments.

Further, computational methods complement experimental approaches by providing access to mechanisms and properties that remain difficult to probe directly.
